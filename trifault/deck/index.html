The engine

The verdict engine.

Every vote is a trit: 0 says it, 1 can't tell, 2 doesn't.

Every statement is a p-trit with energy

Ej(z) = −log πz − Σi log θi[z, vij]

θi is rater i's 3×3 confusion matrix. Block Gibbs alternates statements and raters.

It's Bayesian Dawid–Skene (1979) as p-trit physics.

Grades map by the study's rubric: 9–10 says it, 7–8 can't tell, 0–6 doesn't.

TriFault · SF Lean MeetupDawid & Skene, 1979 · Rubric: Zhang et al., Beyond Compilation, 2026
[22:15–23:30] The verdict engine. Every vote is a trit: 0 means says it, 1 can't tell, 2 doesn't. Every statement j is a p-trit whose energy for state z is minus log of the prior pi z, minus the sum over raters i of log theta i at row z, column v i j, where v i j is rater i's vote on statement j. Theta i is rater i's 3 by 3 confusion matrix: how likely they are to vote each way given the true answer. Block Gibbs sampling alternates: sample every statement given the raters, then every rater's confusion matrix given the statements. Statisticians will recognise this: it's Bayesian Dawid–Skene from 1979, written as p-trit physics. Judge and human scores out of 10 become trits by the study's own rubric: 9 to 10 says it, 7 to 8 can't tell, 0 to 6 doesn't.

Back end · algorithm

A p-bit is a Gibbs sampler in one device.

mi = sgn(tanh(Ii) − r), r ~ U(−1,1)

Ii = I0(hi + Σj Jij mj)

Update one at a time and the chain samples P(m) ∝ e−E(m).

Our verdict model is bipartite: statements, then raters. Two colours, exact block Gibbs.

The catch

“Updating the p-bits in parallel leads to incorrect results.”

Camsari et al., PRX 2017

The fix

Colour the graph. Unconnected p-bits update together, with no approximation.

Chowdhury et al., 2023

TriFault · SF Lean MeetupCamsari, Faria, Sutton & Datta, PRX 2017 · Chowdhury et al., arXiv 2023
[27:00–28:30] A p-bit is a binary unit that is randomly plus or minus 1, with its bias set by an input current. Camsari, Faria, Sutton and Datta, Physical Review X 2017: m_i equals the sign of tanh of I_i minus a uniform random number, and I_i is a weighted sum of the neighbours plus a bias, scaled by I_0, which acts as an inverse temperature. If you update the p-bits one at a time, the network samples the Boltzmann distribution of the energy. That is exactly Gibbs sampling. The catch, in their words: updating the p-bits in parallel leads to incorrect results. The fix, from the full-stack review by Chowdhury et al. 2023: colour the graph, and update all p-bits of one colour together. That is exact. Our verdict model is bipartite: statements only touch raters' confusion matrices, and raters only touch statements. So two colours suffice: sample all statements, then all raters. That's the block Gibbs I described earlier.

[33:00–34:15] Why three states. A current-swept I–V of this switch shows two negative differential resistance regions. The authors attribute the low-current one to "field-triggered thermal runaway", from Joule heating, and the high-current one to "a Mott-transition-related insulator–metal transition". In the oscillator circuit that gives five regimes as you raise the voltage. At 1.290 volts it stays off. At 1.340 it randomly either stays off or starts oscillating. At 1.500 it always oscillates. They attribute it to electrical and thermal noise. Note the path is ordinal: off to oscillating to on. There's no direct off-to-on transition, which comes back as a limitation.

[34:15–35:15] How you read a trit without counting peaks. The metric is first-time-to-oscillate: from the start of the voltage pulse to the first oscillation peak. The readout only asks whether that first peak lands inside a monitoring window, t_mtr, which they set to 2.5 microseconds. Comparator 1 against a 1.5 volt reference says whether the base state is on or off; comparator 2 against 0.8 volts clocks a D flip-flop on the first oscillation edge. Two bits: 00 is off, 01 or 11 is oscillating, 10 is on, mapped to Potts angles 0, 2π/3 and 4π/3. The probability follows 1 minus e to the minus lambda t, where lambda is the transition rate at that bias. Voltage sets the odds; a longer window gives a wider probabilistic voltage range. 2.5 microseconds per trit, 2.4 nanojoules for the oscillator, and the statistics hold with plus or minus 0.2 microseconds of timing jitter. Their circuit was a breadboard prototype with discrete comparators and a microcontroller.

Back end · honest read

What the p-trit paper shows, and what it doesn’t.

Shows

Max-3-Cut, 20 random graphs, N = 14

Error vs brute force: MSE 4.35 vs 6.05 for a greedy p-bit machine

About 30% higher accuracy at about 33% of the operations of p-bit computing.

Doesn’t show

  • The Potts machine used MCU-emulated p-trits. One real device was measured.
  • Its update keeps two neighbouring states: an approximation to Gibbs.
  • No direct on ↔ off path.
  • No endurance or NIST numbers in the main text.
TriFault · SF Lean MeetupRhee et al., Advanced Science 2026 (Figs. 4–5; Conclusions)
[35:15–36:30] What the paper shows. A ternary Potts machine solving Max-3-Cut: on 20 random graphs with 14 vertices and 50 percent connectivity, the mean squared error against brute force was 4.35, versus 6.05 for a greedy p-bit Boltzmann machine that has to do three successive 2-cuts. Their abstract summarises it as about 30 percent higher accuracy at about 33 percent of the computational overhead. They also simulate ternary number partitioning with 50 numbers. What it does not show, and this room will ask. One: the Potts machine ran on microcontroller-emulated p-trits, because packaging and integration of the discrete NbOx devices aren't available yet; only the single p-trit was a real measured device. Two: their sampling rule, segmented probabilistic sampling, gives non-zero probability only to the two neighbouring states and sets the farthest to zero. That's an approximation to Gibbs, and the main text has no convergence proof for the ternary case. Three: the off-oscillate-on path is ordinal; there's no direct on-to-off transition, which they say slows convergence. Four: the main text reports no endurance or NIST randomness tests; those may be in the supplement, which I couldn't access.

TriFault · SF Lean MeetupBerman et al., arXiv 2023 (Table II) · Eyert 2002 · Wahila et al., PRM 2019 · Brito et al., PRB 2017
[38:30–39:30] Why does pairing make an insulator? There's a real debate. Peierls: niobium has one d electron per atom; pairing along the chain splits the d-parallel band into bonding and antibonding halves and opens a gap. Eyert showed that plain DFT already opens one; Wahila et al. 2019 with hard X-ray photoemission and EXAFS conclude a second-order Peierls transition "with little to no contribution from electron correlations". On the other side, Brito, Aguiar, Haule and Kotliar, PRB 2017, with cluster DMFT: "not a purely Peierls-type insulator"; non-local correlations inside the dimers help open the gap, but correlations are weaker than in VO2. Berman et al. 2023 sit in the middle. The gap numbers show why it's contested: LDA 0.35 eV, DMFT 0.73, optical 0.85, tunnelling 1.05 plus or minus 0.29, HSE 1.48. The device papers, including KAIST's, call the switch Mott-related. For the hardware it doesn't change much; for this room it's a nice example of a statement whose meaning depends on who's saying it.
