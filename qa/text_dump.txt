=== Slide 1 [TITLE]
  - Atom-by-Atom Silicon: Building Quantum Processors from Single Dopant Atoms
  - Device profile: STM-patterned P and As dopant qubits, featuring the work of Neil J. Curson (UCL)
  - Hanah Rahman · EE143 · Fall 2026
  - AI disclosure: AI tools were used to draft and lay out these slides and to code the original diagrams.
  - A 2×2 array of single As atoms: lithography → AsH₃ dose → anneal. Reproduced from Stock et al., Adv. Mater. 36, 2312282 (2024), CC BY 4.0
  NOTES: Hi everyone. Today's device is a quantum processor where every qubit is a single dopant atom, put into silicon one at a time with a scanning tunnelling microscope. You already know every ingredient from EE143: a clean surface, a resist, lithography, gas-phase doping, an activation anneal, epitaxy, etch and metal. The twist is that the resist is one atom thick and the 'implant' is one molecule. On the right is real STM data: a 2×2 array of single arsenic atoms, imaged after each of three steps. The person behind that image is Neil Curson at UCL, so I'll follow his work through the process. Likely question: is this a real device or a demo? It's a real fabricated array, imaged at each step.

  - Kane-style architecture: each ³¹P donor sits under an A-gate; J-gates between donors tune their coupling. Schematic after Kane1998 (original drawing, not to scale).
  - EE143 lens: it's a doped-Si device whose 'diffusions' are single atoms and whose wiring is δ-doped silicon.
  - Schofield2025 §3.2, §5.4; Stock2024; Constantinou2023; Fuechsle2012; Kane1998

  NOTES: Here's the spec sheet. The substrate is ordinary Si(001), switched to silicon-28 for the qubit devices. The dopants are phosphorus, from phosphine, or arsenic, from arsine. The headline is placement: plus or minus one atomic spacing. The wires are δ-layers, sheets around 10^14 per square centimetre that are less than a nanometre thick. Everything is measured at millikelvin. On the right is the original idea, Kane 1998: one phosphorus donor under each A-gate, with J-gates between them to turn coupling on and off. The number to remember: ±1 atomic spacing. Likely question: why silicon-28? The record results in this talk, 35.6 s coherence and the 2025 processors, were all in ²⁸Si; later I'll flag why that matters when comparing fidelities.

  NOTES: Thirty years in one line. 1994: Lyding shows an STM tip can pull hydrogen off silicon, which gives us lithography. 1998: Kane proposes phosphorus nuclear spins as qubits. 2003: the first single phosphorus atoms placed on purpose, Curson among the authors. 2012: a transistor whose active region is one atom. 2019: the first two-qubit gate between donors and a 3D stacked device. 2020 and 2024: Curson's group adds arsenic and pushes single-atom yield to 97%, and also shows EUV light can do the hydrogen removal. 2025: a four-qubit Grover search and an 11-qubit processor. Likely question: what changed in 2012? The atom became a working device, wired up and measured as a transistor, not just imaged.

=== Slide 4 [TWO_FIG]
  - Why a single donor makes a good qubit
  - P nucleus (I = 1/2) + its electron = 4 spin states / Electron Bohr radius ~2 nm; exchange depends on exact donor position, so atomic precision matters / In ²⁸Si: coherence up to 35.6 s and single-qubit gates close to 99.99% (ion-implanted donors)
  - Spin levels, schematic (ours)
  - Donor control and readout: SET charge sensor + on-chip microwave antenna (a); ion-implanted device (b). Reproduced from Schofield et al., Nano Futures 9, 012001 (2025) §3.2 Fig. 1, CC BY 4.0
  - Schofield2025 §1, §3.2
  - 4
  NOTES: Why bother with single atoms? A phosphorus donor brings two spins for free: its nucleus with spin one-half and its bound electron. Two times two gives four states, shown in my sketch on the left; we drive the electron with ESR and the nucleus with NMR. On the right is how you read it out: a single-electron transistor senses charge, spin-dependent tunnelling turns spin into charge, and an on-chip antenna delivers the microwaves. In silicon-28, coherence reaches 35.6 seconds, which is enormous for a qubit. The catch is the electron's Bohr radius of about 2 nm: the exchange coupling between two donors depends on their exact lattice positions. That's why fabrication precision is the whole game. Likely question: what's ESR? Electron spin resonance, flipping the electron with microwaves.

=== Slide 5 [TABLE]
  - Why not just ion-implant the donors?
  -  | Ion implantation | STM H-resist lithography | Placement | Sub-100 nm routine; Kane-type devices need ≈15 nm | ±1 atomic spacing | One dopant per site | 36% (Poisson, without ion counting) | 97 ± 2% (As, 4-site window)
  - Deterministic single-ion implanter column. Reproduced from Schofield et al., Nano Futures 9, 012001 (2025) §2.4 Fig. 2, CC BY 4.0
  - Schofield2025 §1, §2.4; Stock2024. Poisson bars: P(k)=e⁻¹/k!, our calculation of the 36% quoted in Schofield2025
  - 5
  NOTES: This is the obvious EE143 question: we implant dopants every day, so why an STM? Two reasons in the table. Placement: implantation is routinely sub-100 nm, but a Kane-type device needs about 15 nm, and the STM gives plus or minus one atomic spacing. Second, counting: ions arrive randomly. If you aim for one ion per site on average, Poisson statistics say only 36% of sites get exactly one; the bar chart is that distribution, which I calculated. Deterministic implanters like the one on the right add ion detection to fix this. Compare that 36% with the 97% Curson's group gets for arsenic. Likely question: can't you just detect each ion? Yes, that's the ion-counting route, and it's an active alternative.

  NOTES: Steps one to three make a perfect surface: clean and outgas at 600 °C for more than eight hours, flash to about 1200 °C, then cap every surface atom with hydrogen.   NOTES: Step one is about cleanliness, because a single stray atom is a defect at this scale. Samples get an acetone and IPA clean, then outgas for more than eight hours at 600 °C, then repeated flash anneals to about 1200 °C, all in ultra-high vacuum below 5×10^-11 millibar. Then atomic hydrogen goes onto every surface silicon. My render on the right shows the result: rows of silicon dimers, each atom capped by one hydrogen. That monolayer is the resist. Think of it as spin-coating, except the thickness is exactly one atom and perfectly uniform. The number to remember: one atom thick. Likely question: why flash to 1200 °C? It's the standard surface prep in these papers; the pack gives the recipe, not the mechanism.

=== Slide 10 [PROCESS]
  - Step 5 · Dosing: phosphine vs arsine
  - Step 5 of 11
  - PH₃ breaks down stepwise, PH₂+H → PH+2H → P+3H, over minutes / AsH₃ dissociates three orders of magnitude faster: tens of seconds / Single-atom dose: AsH₃ at 5×10⁻⁹ mbar for 10 min; As saturates at 0.24 ± 0.04 ML
  - Si substrate
  - Si cap
  - H resist
  - P
  - As
  - Al
  - SiO₂
  - Fragments in each window. Original render.
  - Window array before/after AsH₃. Reproduced from Stock et al., Adv. Mater. 36, 2312282 (2024) Fig. 3, CC BY 4.0
  - Stock2020 (citing Wilson2004); Stock2024
  - 10
  NOTES: Now we expose the whole sample to a dopant gas, like a pre-deposition step, except molecules only stick in the bare windows. Phosphine is slow: it sheds hydrogens one at a time, PH2 plus H, then PH plus two H, then P plus three H, over minutes, and each fragment needs a free site. Arsine is the key difference: it dissociates three orders of magnitude faster, in tens of seconds, so it needs fewer sites and fewer things go wrong. The single-atom recipe is arsine at 5×10^-9 millibar for ten minutes. Arsenic saturates at 0.24 monolayer. Bottom right: a window array before and after dosing. Likely question: why does faster dissociation help? Arsenic gets by with a four-site window instead of six, so there are fewer ways to fail.

  - Step 6 of 11
  - 350 °C anneal (1–2 min), below the 380 °C onset of H₂ desorption, so the H resist survives / Each incorporated As ejects one Si atom / So counting ejected Si counts the dopants: built-in metrology

  - Stock2020; Stock2024

  NOTES: Step six is the drive-in, but tiny. A 350 °C anneal for one to two minutes lets the dopant swap into the top silicon layer. Why 350? Hydrogen starts leaving the surface at 380 °C, so we stay just under it and the resist survives everywhere else. On the right, real data: a patterned square through dose and anneal, with clean resist outside. There's a neat bonus: each arsenic that goes in kicks out one silicon atom, which sits on the surface as an adatom. Count adatoms and you've counted dopants, metrology for free. The number to remember: 350 °C versus 380 °C. Likely question: can you overshoot? Above 380 °C hydrogen starts to desorb, so the resist stops protecting the rest of the surface.

  NOTES: Steps seven and eight bury the dopants: a classic thermal-budget fight. Low temperature keeps atoms in place; high temperature gives good crystal. For phosphorus the answer is a constant 250 °C overgrowth, with about 5 nm of segregation and full activation. For arsenic, Curson's group grows a thin unheated locking layer, does a quick 500 °C, ten-second RTA, then continues epitaxy. A worked example: 2 nm unheated, 500 °C for 15 seconds, then 28 nm at 250 °C, so the layer sits 30 nm down. The SIMS profile on the right shows the arsenic still in a sharp spike. Likely question: why not grow everything hot? That's the trade-off on the slide: even at 250 °C phosphorus segregates about 5 nm.

=== Slide 14 [TWO_FIG]
  - Did it work? Measuring a buried δ-layer
  - As δ-layer: 1.6 ± 0.1 kΩ/□, 28 ± 1 cm²/V·s; 81% activation (88% at 560 °C); P layers reach 2.4×10¹⁴ cm⁻² / ARPES: layers <1 nm thick; As confines better than P / XRF at ≈1 µm: <0.2 Å As motion, <3% loss of active dopants
  - EE143 check (our calc.):  Rₛ = 1/(q·Nₛ·μ)  →  Nₛ = 1/(1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C × 1600 Ω × 28 cm²/V·s) ≈ 1.4×10¹⁴ cm⁻², consistent with the ≈10¹⁴ cm⁻² sheets.
  - ARPES Fermi surfaces of P (top) and As (bottom) δ-layers. Reproduced from Constantinou et al., Adv. Sci. 10, 2302101 (2023) Fig. 2, CC BY 4.0
  - Mobility, carrier density and Rₛ vs RTA temperature. Reproduced from Stock et al., ACS Nano 14, 3316 (2020) Fig. 4f,g, CC BY 4.0
  - Stock2020; Constantinou2023; DAnna2023. Nₛ check is our calculation from Stock2020's numbers
  - 14
  NOTES: Did the process work? Three kinds of evidence. Electrical: an arsenic δ-layer measures 1.6 kilo-ohms per square with mobility of 28, and 81% of the arsenic is active after the 500 °C RTA, 88% at 560. The plots at bottom right track mobility, density and sheet resistance against RTA temperature. My EE143 sanity check: sheet resistance equals one over q times sheet density times mobility. Plug in 1.6 kΩ and 28 and you get about 1.4×10^14 per square centimetre, right where it should be. Second, X-ray ARPES, top right, shows the layers are under a nanometre thick, and arsenic is tighter than phosphorus. Third, X-ray fluorescence imaging moved the arsenic less than 0.2 Å. Likely question: does the X-ray beam change the device? Less than 3% loss of active dopants.

  - P 0.7¹⁶ < 0.4%

  NOTES: Here's the problem that motivated Curson's arsenic work. Phosphorus lands in an ideal three-dimer window only 63 ± 10% of the time; Stock 2024 says less than 70% in six-site windows. That sounds fine until you need many sites at once. Array yield multiplies: 0.7 to the n. The orange line falls to under 0.4% by 16 atoms. The teal line is the same formula with arsenic's 97%, still about 61% at 16. On the right I simulated 4×4 arrays: the phosphorus row is full of red failures, the arsenic row has mostly perfect boxed arrays. This is the same logic as die yield in a fab: defects multiply. Number to remember: 0.7^16 < 0.4%. Likely question: can you repair a failed site? Possibly, by repeating lithography and dosing.

  NOTES: Because arsine dissociates so fast and needs fewer sites, a window of just four lattice sites, two dimers, is enough. In that window a single arsenic atom incorporates 97 ± 2% of the time. That window is one site in four, about 0.6 square nanometres, which is 33% better placement precision than phosphorus. === Slide 20 [TWO_FIG]
  - Same atoms, new job: a quantum simulator
  - A 3×3 array of dopant-based quantum dots, written by the same STM process / Tuned from metallic toward Mott-insulating behaviour: a hardware model of interacting electrons
  - EE143 view: the 'circuit' here is a lattice of doped islands with in-plane δ-layer gates and leads.
  - 3×3 dopant-dot array: schematic (a) and STM images (b, c). Reproduced from Wang et al., Nat. Commun. 13, 6824 (2022) Fig. 1, CC BY 4.0
  - Wang2022
  - 20
  NOTES: Same fabrication, different job. Instead of qubits, this group wrote a 3×3 grid of tiny dopant-based quantum dots, shown in the schematic and the STM images on the right, with gates and leads written in the same layer. Electrons hopping between dots behave like electrons in a crystal, so the device simulates a material physics model directly in hardware. By changing the array, they tuned it from metallic behaviour toward a Mott insulator, where repulsion between electrons stops conduction. For us the interesting part is that it's a circuit made only of doped islands and δ-layer wiring. Likely question: why not simulate on a computer? Interacting-electron problems get exponentially hard as the lattice grows.

=== Slide 21 [TWO_FIG]
  - The throughput wall
  - STM: 10–1000 nm/s; precise mode ≈100 atoms/s / EUV: equivalent ≈10¹⁴ nm/s / Even 10⁷ MEMS tips over a 300 mm wafer stay orders of magnitude too slow for CMOS volumes
  - Schofield2025 §2.2, §5.4
  - 21
  NOTES: Here's the wall. The STM writes at 10 nm per second for atom-wide lines, up to about 1000 nm per second at 2 volts and hundreds of nanoamps. In atomically precise mode that's around 100 atoms a second. EUV lithography, as used in CMOS fabs, writes at an equivalent of about 10^14 nm per second, eleven orders of magnitude faster. Note the log axis. People have proposed massively parallel MEMS tip arrays, up to ten million tips over a 300 mm wafer, and the roadmap says even that stays orders of magnitude too slow for CMOS volumes. The number to remember: eleven orders of magnitude. Likely question: so is this a dead end? Not for qubit cores, which is where the hybrid idea next comes in.

=== Slide 22 [TWO_FIG]
  - Scaling: EUV photons instead of a tip
  - First H removal from Si(001):H with monochromatic EUV (109 eV), driven by secondary electrons / Doses up to 18 000 J/cm²: several minutes per chip with 300 W sources / Hybrid flow: STM for qubits, EUV for contacts, gates and wiring
  - Proposed 4-stage UHV flow combining EUV and STM lithography. Reproduced from Constantinou et al., Nat. Commun. 15, 694 (2024) Fig. 5, CC BY 4.0
  - Resistless EUV lines, 75 nm half-pitch. Schofield2025 §5.3 Fig. 1 (adapted from Tseng2023), CC BY 4.0
  - Constantinou2024; Schofield2025 §5.3; Tseng2023
  - 22
  NOTES: Curson's 2024 Nature Communications paper showed for the first time that monochromatic EUV light at 109 eV removes hydrogen from the same silicon surface; it's mostly the secondary electrons from the silicon that do the work. It needs doses up to 18,000 joules per square centimetre, several minutes per chip with a 300 watt source, but it's parallel. The proposed flow on the right uses the same hydrogen resist: EUV patterns the big contacts and wiring, the STM writes the qubit cores, then dose and encapsulate as before. The SEM bottom-left shows resistless EUV lines at 75 nm half-pitch. Likely question: why not normal photoresist? The proposed flow keeps the hydrogen monolayer as the resist so every stage stays in UHV, and Tseng 2023 patterned with no photoresist at all.

=== Slide 23 [TWO_FIG]
  - Our prototype: a 2×2 arsenic register
  - Concept render (ours, not to scale): dark pillars are Al contacts. Layout after the gated As array in Stock2024 Fig. 1.
  - Array yield pⁿ (our model); P and As curves use the measured 0.7 and 0.97.
  - Four single As atoms in 4-site windows; in-plane As δ-layer source, drain and gates; Al vias / Design rule from pⁿ: 16 sites at p = 0.97 give ≈61% perfect arrays; at p = 0.7, ≈0.3%
  - Our design. Inputs: Stock2024 (97 ± 2%, 4-site window, pⁿ); Stock2020; DAnna2023
  - 23
  NOTES: To pull it together, here's a prototype we designed on paper, using only recipes from the papers. Four single arsenic atoms in four-site windows form a 2×2 register, with source, drain and two gates written as arsenic δ-layers in the same plane, buried under epitaxial silicon and reached by aluminium vias. The 3D surface on the right is our yield model, p to the n. It shows why the dopant choice decides the design: at arsenic's 0.97 a 16-site array comes out perfect about 61% of the time, at phosphorus's 0.7 about 0.3%. So the register size you can afford is set by chemistry, not by lithography resolution. Likely question: did you build it? No, it's a design exercise; the next slide is its traveler.

  NOTES: And here's the traveler for that prototype, every step with its recipe and the EE143 module it maps to. It's a normal process flow: clean and bake, coat a resist, write it, pre-deposit dopant from the gas phase, drive in, RTA, epitaxy, alignment marks, etch, metal, and a Hall measurement. On the right I plotted each step's peak temperature. The two hot steps, outgas and the 1200 °C flash, happen before any patterning. After the pattern exists, nothing exceeds the 500 °C, 15-second RTA. That's the design principle: front-load the heat, then protect the atoms. Likely question: where does it break? Step 4 throughput, which is why the EUV hybrid matters.

  - Curson's 4-site window beats P's <70%; that's what makes arrays possible.
  - 3
  - Throughput is the next wall; EUV is the bridge.
  - Same resist, photons for wiring, STM for qubits.

  - 2020
  - 2024
  - 2025

  NOTES: Three things to take home. One: placing single atoms works, using the same steps you know, a resist, lithography, gas-phase doping and an anneal, at the scale of one atom. Two: arsenic brought single-atom yield to about 97%, versus under 70% for phosphorus, and because array yield multiplies, that's the difference between arrays that work and arrays that don't. That's Neil Curson's group's key result. Three: throughput is the next wall, eleven orders of magnitude behind EUV, and the bridge is to use EUV on the same hydrogen resist for everything except the qubit cores.
