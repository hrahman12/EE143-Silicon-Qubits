 NOTES: This talk is about a transistor-like device whose active region is one atom: a phosphorus or arsenic donor in silicon, placed with a scanning tunnelling microscope. Physically, the donor is a hydrogen atom embedded in a semiconductor: one extra electron bound by a screened Coulomb potential. In EE143 terms, every step is a process you know, a resist, lithography, gas-phase doping, an activation anneal, epitaxy, etch and metal, scaled down to a resist one atom thick and a dose of one molecule. The render behind me is the diamond-cubic lattice, a = 5.431 Å, with a 2×2 arsenic register. Likely question: is the image data? No, it's a render; every micrograph later is measured.

 NOTES: Three quantities run through the whole talk. Precision: one lattice site on Si(001) is 0.384 nm, and that is the placement error we can achieve. Yield: if each site works with probability p, an n-site array works with probability p to the n, so p must be very close to one. Speed: a tip writes about 100 atoms per second, which sets the throughput limit. Act one asks why a single donor is a good qubit, act two builds it, act three tests it, from conductance to entanglement, and act four confronts the write-speed problem. Keep the question in mind: how do you put exactly one atom exactly where you want it?
=== Slide 3
 - A single donor atom is a natural qubit
 - ACT 1 · WHY SINGLE ATOMS
 - P nucleus (I = 1/2) + its electron = 4 spin states / Electron Bohr radius ~2 nm; exchange depends on exact donor position, so atomic precision matters / In ²⁸Si: coherence up to 35.6 s and single-qubit gates close to 99.99% (ion-implanted donors)
 - Spin levels, schematic (ours)
 - Donor control and readout: SET charge sensor + on-chip microwave antenna (a); ion-implanted device (b). Reproduced from Schofield et al., Nano Futures 9, 012001 (2025) §3.2 Fig. 1, CC BY 4.0
 - Schofield2025 §1, §3.2
 - 3
 NOTES: Treat the donor as hydrogen in a dielectric. The effective Bohr radius is a_B = ε_r (m_e/m*) a_0, so silicon's large permittivity and small effective mass stretch hydrogen's 0.053 nm to about 2 nm. Two spins come for free: the electron and the phosphorus nucleus with I = 1/2, giving a 2 × 2 = 4-level system. Electron transitions are driven by ESR, nuclear ones by NMR. Readout converts spin to charge: a spin-up electron can tunnel into a nearby single-electron transistor, a spin-down one cannot, and the SET current changes. In isotopically purified ²⁸Si there are no stray nuclear spins, which is why ion-implanted donors there reach 35.6 s coherence and gates near 99.99%. Likely question: is it really hydrogen-like? To first order; silicon's six conduction-band valleys split the ground state.
=== Slide 4
 - Why one site matters: wavefunction overlap
 - ACT 1 · WHY SINGLE ATOMS
 - To scale: two P donors 13.0 nm apart over the Si(001) surface lattice, hydrogenic envelope |ψ|² ∝ e^(−2r/a_B) with a_B ≈ 2 nm (our model; inputs He2019, Schofield2025 §3.2, Stock2024).
 - Each spin is a qubit: |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ on the Bloch sphere (schematic).
 - Qubits interact through exchange, set by how their electron clouds overlap / Exchange depends strongly on exact donor position (valley oscillations), so a one-site error changes the gate
 - Schofield2025 §3.2; He2019; Stock2024. Envelope plot is our model, not a full valley calculation
 - 4
 NOTES: Two-qubit gates use exchange, J, which comes from wavefunction overlap. For hydrogenic envelopes, |ψ|² falls as e^(−2r/a_B), so overlap, and roughly J, decays exponentially with donor spacing R. A quick estimate: moving one donor by one lattice site, 0.384 nm, multiplies the envelope term by e^(−0.384), about 0.68, a 30% change in coupling from a single-site error, before any valley effects. Silicon's six conduction-band valleys add interference that makes J oscillate site to site, which is why precision matters even more. The map is drawn to scale: two donors 13 nm apart on the real surface lattice. Likely question: can't you tune J with gates? Yes, but only within a window set by placement.
=== Slide 5
 - The problem: implantation can't place one atom
 - ACT 1 · WHY SINGLE ATOMS
 TABLE:  | Ion implantation | STM H-resist lithography | Placement | Sub-100 nm routine; Kane-type devices need ≈15 nm | ±1 atomic spacing | One dopant per site | 36% (Poisson, without ion counting) | 97 ± 2% (As, 4-site window) | Best ion counting | 99.85 ± 0.02% detection confidence, 14 keV ³¹P⁺; yield limit 98.1% (Jakob2022) | Placement still set by ion straggling, not by the lattice
 CHART: Ions per site at mean 1 ion/site: exactly one only ~36% [('Probability', [36.8, 36.8, 18.4, 6.1, 1.9])]
 - Deterministic single-ion implanter column. Reproduced from Schofield et al., Nano Futures 9, 012001 (2025) §2.4 Fig. 2, CC BY 4.0
 - Schofield2025 §1, §2.4; Stock2024; Jakob2022. Poisson bars: P(k)=e⁻¹/k!, our calculation of the 36% in Schofield2025
 - 5
 NOTES: Ion implantation is a Poisson process. If the mean number of ions per site is λ, the probability of exactly one is λe^(−λ). Differentiate: the maximum is at λ = 1, where P(1) = 1/e ≈ 36.8%. So without counting ions, no choice of dose gives better than 37% single occupancy; the bars are that distribution. Even with near-perfect ion detection, 99.85% at 14 keV, the stopping position is random: straggle spreads each ion over nanometres. Placement is set by ion–solid scattering statistics, not by the crystal lattice. STM placement is lattice-registered, ±1 site, and arsenic windows give 97 ± 2% single occupancy. Likely question: what about lower energy? Straggle shrinks but never reaches one lattice site.

 NOTES: Surface preparation. An outgas above 8 hours at 600 °C and flashes to about 1200 °C desorb the native oxide and carbon and leave the 2×1 dimer reconstruction. All in ultra-high vacuum below 5×10⁻¹¹ mbar. Why so low? Kinetic theory: at 10⁻⁶ Torr a surface collects about a monolayer per second, so at 10⁻¹¹ mbar a monolayer takes on the order of a day. Then atomic hydrogen at 330 °C, 5×10⁻⁷ mbar for 5 minutes, terminates every dangling bond. That monolayer is the resist: chemically inert where present, reactive where removed. Its contrast ratio is set by chemistry, not by optics. Likely question: why 330 °C? Hot enough for ordered monohydride, below hydrogen desorption.

 NOTES: STM lithography is controlled tunnelling. Current scales as I ∝ V e^(−2κd), with κ = √(2mφ)/ħ ≈ 1 Å⁻¹ for a barrier of a few eV, so each ångström of tip height changes current by roughly e², 7 to 8 times. That exponential sensitivity is why the tip resolves single atoms. Writing uses higher bias to break Si–H bonds by electron excitation. Check the line dose: current over speed, 10 nA ÷ 10 nm/s = 1 C/m, which is the quoted 10⁻² C/cm. Arsenic windows use 3.5 V and 3000 pA at 50 nm/s, two dimers each, on a 4.6 nm pitch, twelve lattice vectors. Likely question: e-beam analogy? Yes; here dose is charge per length, and the resist is one atom.

 NOTES: Dosing is gas-surface kinetics. The impingement flux is Φ = p/√(2πmkT). For arsine, mass 78 amu, at 5×10⁻⁹ mbar and 300 K, that gives about 9×10¹¹ molecules per cm² per second. Over 10 minutes, about 5×10¹⁴ arrivals per cm², roughly 0.8 per surface site. So every window sees order one molecule, and the hydrogen resist rejects the rest. Phosphine then loses hydrogens stepwise over minutes, needing free sites for each fragment; arsine dissociates about a thousand times faster, in tens of seconds. Saturated arsenic coverage is 0.24 ± 0.04 monolayer, a quarter of the sites. Likely question: why not a full monolayer? Coverage stops at 0.24 ML whatever the dose rate, so surface chemistry, not gas supply, sets the limit.

 NOTES: Incorporation is an activated process with a thermal window. At 350 °C, kT ≈ 54 meV: enough for the arsenic or phosphorus to exchange into the top silicon layer, but below the 380 °C onset where hydrogen starts desorbing and the resist fails. A 30-kelvin window separates working from failing. Each incorporated arsenic ejects exactly one silicon atom to the surface, so the reaction is a substitution, As in, Si out, and counting ejected silicon adatoms counts dopants. That is in-situ metrology at single-atom resolution. In EE143 terms this is the drive-in and activation anneal, with a diffusion length of essentially zero. Likely question: what limits the window? The Si–H bond desorption barrier.

 NOTES: Burying the dopants is a thermal-budget optimisation. Epitaxial quality wants higher temperature, dopant confinement wants lower. Phosphorus grown over at a constant 250 °C segregates about 5 nm toward the surface. For arsenic: 2 nm at room temperature to lock the atoms, a 500 °C, 15-second RTA that leaves a confined, electrically active layer, then 28 nm at 250 °C. At 0.003 nm/s, 28 nm takes 9333 seconds, about 2.6 hours. Bulk diffusion using the textbook Arrhenius parameters gives 2√(Dt) around 10⁻⁴ nm even for the RTA step, so any broadening comes from surface segregation during growth, not bulk diffusion. Likely question: why not grow everything cold? Below a critical thickness cold silicon grows epitaxially; beyond it, the film turns amorphous.

 NOTES: Yield is a product of probabilities. If each site independently succeeds with probability p, an n-site array succeeds with p^n. Phosphorus in an ideal three-dimer window: 63 ± 10%, below 70% in six-site windows. Then 0.7¹⁶ is 0.33%, and for fifty donors 0.63⁵⁰ is about 10⁻¹⁰. Invert the formula: the largest array with 50% yield is n = ln 0.5 / ln p, about 2 sites for phosphorus and 23 for arsenic at 0.97. The uncertainty band shows the sensitivity: arsenic at 95 to 99% gives 44 to 85% for sixteen sites. Likely question: are failures independent? The formula assumes it; correlated failures would change the statistics.

 NOTES: The arsenic solution is a geometric design rule. Arsine's fast dissociation means one molecule needs fewer free sites, so a four-site window, two dimers, admits exactly one. Single-atom yield is 97 ± 2%. The window covers one in four lattice sites, about 0.6 nm², giving 33% better positional precision than phosphorus.  NOTES: The same chemistry generalises. On germanium, arsine incorporates at room temperature with no anneal, and hydrogen leaves by 330 °C.  NOTES: Entanglement is verified by quantum state tomography: measure in many bases and reconstruct the density matrix ρ. For a Bell state, ρ has four equal corner elements of ½ and nothing else; fidelity is F = ⟨ψ|ρ|ψ⟩. All three nuclear pairs reach 96.8 to 97.7%. === Slide 24
 - Same atoms, new job: a quantum simulator
 - ACT 3 · DOES IT WORK?
 - 2022: 3×3 dopant dots, 2 ± 1 P each, spacing 4.1, 6.6, 10.7 ± 0.3 nm: metallic → Mott-insulating / 2022: 10-dot chain, ~25 P per dot, ≤10 nm apart, 40 nm cap at 0.125 nm/min: topological states
 - EE143 view: the 'circuit' here is a lattice of doped islands with in-plane δ-layer gates and leads.
 - 3×3 dopant-dot array. Wang et al., Nat. Commun. 13, 6824 (2022) Fig. 1, CC BY 4.0
 - 10-dot chain with 6 in-plane gates (STM, 50 nm scale in original). Kiczynski et al., Nature 606, 694 (2022) Fig. 2a, CC BY 4.0

 NOTES: Dopant arrays also simulate many-body physics. The Hubbard Hamiltonian, H = −t Σ c†c + U Σ n↑n↓, has two energies: hopping t, set by tunnelling between dots, and on-site repulsion U. Since t falls exponentially with spacing, changing a 3×3 lattice from 4.1 to 10.7 nm tunes U/t from small, metallic, to large, Mott-insulating, where repulsion freezes the electrons. A 10-dot chain with alternating couplings realises the SSH model: when intracell hopping is weaker than intercell hopping, the band is topological, with edge states pinned at zero energy, seen as 2 conductance peaks instead of 10. Likely question: why dopants? Lithographic precision sets t directly.
=== Slide 25
 - The next wall: throughput
 - ACT 4 · HOW IT SCALES
 CHART: EUV writes 11 orders of magnitude faster than STM [('write speed (nm/s)', [10.0, 1000.0, 100000000000000.0])]
 - STM: 10–1000 nm/s; precise mode ≈100 atoms/s / EUV: equivalent ≈10¹⁴ nm/s / Even 10⁷ MEMS tips over a 300 mm wafer stay orders of magnitude too slow for CMOS volumes
 - Schofield2025 §2.2, §5.4
 - 25
 NOTES: Throughput is an EE scaling problem. A tip in precise mode writes about 100 atoms per second. One square centimetre of Si(001) has 6.78×10¹⁴ surface sites, so writing it serially takes 6.8×10¹² seconds, about 215,000 years. Faster modes reach 1000 nm per second but lose atomic precision. EUV lithography exposes the equivalent of 10¹⁴ nm per second, eleven orders of magnitude faster, because every photon in the field exposes in parallel. Even ten million tips across a 300 mm wafer remain orders of magnitude too slow for chip volumes. The answer has to be parallel exposure for everything except the qubit cores. Likely question: can tips run faster? Speed trades directly against precision.
=== Slide 26
 - Scaling: EUV photons instead of a tip
 - ACT 4 · HOW IT SCALES
 - First H removal from Si(001):H with monochromatic EUV (109 eV), driven by secondary electrons / Doses up to 18 000 J/cm²: several minutes per chip with 300 W sources / Hybrid flow: STM for qubits, EUV for contacts, gates and wiring
 - Proposed 4-stage UHV flow combining EUV and STM lithography. Reproduced from Constantinou et al., Nat. Commun. 15, 694 (2024) Fig. 5, CC BY 4.0
 - Resistless EUV lines, 75 nm half-pitch. Schofield2025 §5.3 Fig. 1 (adapted from Tseng2023), CC BY 4.0
 - Constantinou2024; Schofield2025 §5.3; Tseng2023
 - 26
 NOTES: EUV can strip the same hydrogen resist. Photon energy E = hc/λ: industrial 13.5 nm light is 92 eV, and hydrogen removal was shown at 109 eV. The mechanism is indirect: photons are absorbed in the silicon, which emits secondary electrons, and those electrons break Si–H bonds, the same bond-breaking physics as under the STM tip. The cost is dose: up to 18,000 J/cm², several minutes per chip with a 300 W source. Resistless EUV gratings at 75 nm half-pitch show the photon route works without organic resist. The proposed flow: EUV for leads and wiring, STM for qubit cores. Likely question: resolution? Diffraction, R = k₁λ/NA, limits EUV to roughly ten nanometres.
